A good cook decides more than half of the result before the fire is lit, simply by looking at the raw meat. This page teaches that reading: what you see, what you feel and what each sign means.

## Colour: three states of the same molecule

The colour of beef comes mainly from **myoglobin**, the protein that stores oxygen inside the muscle. It changes according to the chemistry at the surface:

- **Purplish red**, immediately after cutting, when the myoglobin has not yet met oxygen. A cut that has just been opened, or that arrived vacuum-packed, has this colour. It is not a defect.
- **Bright (cherry) red**, a few minutes after the cut has been exposed to air. This is oxygenated myoglobin, the colour we associate with fresh meat.
- **Brown**, when the iron in the myoglobin oxidises. In a refrigerated display this happens after a few days. Brown at the surface does not, in itself, mean the meat has spoiled; it means it has been exposed for a long time. Smell and texture may reveal signs of deterioration, but they cannot, on their own, confirm that the meat is safe to eat: the absence of an off smell is no guarantee of safety, which depends above all on the cold chain and the expiry date.

This behaviour is described in meat-science textbooks such as *Meat Science and Nutrition* (eCampus Ontario / BCcampus), which also notes that beef and lamb contain more myoglobin than pork, veal or poultry, and are therefore darker.

**In a restaurant**, meat often arrives vacuum-packed or aged, and the colour at the surface is deceptive. What you assess is the freshly cut interior, after a few minutes in the air.

### The warning sign: dark, firm, dry meat

When an animal arrives at the abattoir tired or stressed, it uses up the glycogen reserves in its muscle. Without glycogen, the pH of the meat does not fall as it should after slaughter. The result is what is known as **DFD** meat (*dark, firm, dry*), called a *dark cutter* in the trade. It is dark, tacky at the surface, never develops a bright red colour and keeps less well. For a demanding restaurant, it goes back.

## Fat: where the flavour lives

There are three fat deposits that a professional tells apart:

1. **Cover fat** (the fat on top of a *lombo* (loin), for example). It should be firm and white to cream. A strong yellowish tone is common in older animals reared on pasture, because of the carotenoids in grass. It is not a defect; it is, in fact, a sign of a pasture-based diet.
2. **Intermuscular fat**, between the muscles. It melts and lubricates during cooking.
3. **Intramuscular fat (the "marbling")**, the fine white threads inside the muscle. This is the fat that counts most towards the flavour and juiciness of a grilled steak.

Marbling varies greatly with breed, age and rearing system, and **ageing** intensifies the flavour that the fat carries (see [the ageing page](../02-maturacao/)). How each Portuguese breed behaves in these respects is a matter of ongoing research: it will be published only when there is a source.

## Fibre and texture

Look at the cross-section. A **fine, dense** fibre indicates a muscle that has done little work, such as the *lombinho* (the small loin, inside the *lombo*), and tender meat. A **coarse fibre with plenty of visible connective tissue** indicates a working muscle, such as the *pá* (shoulder), the *peito* (breast) or the *chambão* (leg, shin), which calls for long cooking.

Press the cut with the pad of your finger. It should feel firm, springy and slightly moist, without leaving a milky liquid or a slimy film.

## Smell

Fresh beef has little smell, and a clean one: faintly metallic and milky. Dry-aged beef has aromas of walnut, cured cheese, mushroom and, in long ageing, of ripe fruit and broth. Sweet-sour, sour or ammonia-like smells are a sign of deterioration.

## How to buy: what the label must say

In the European Union, beef must be labelled with traceability information under Regulation (EC) No 1760/2000: a reference number, the country of birth, of fattening and of slaughter, and the approval number of the abattoir and of the cutting plant. If the butcher cannot tell you where the cut comes from, there is a serious failing.

The age and category of the animal (*vitelo* (calf), *novilho* (young bullock), *boi* (ox), *vaca* (cow)) make an enormous difference to flavour. The European carcass classification system (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/1182) grades conformation (S, E, U, R, O, P) and fat cover (from 1 to 5). It is a commercial tool and not an index of gastronomic quality, but it helps in understanding what you are buying.

## The cook's checklist

- Colour of the interior: bright red after a few minutes in the air.
- Surface moist but not slimy.
- Fat firm, with no rancid smell.
- Clean smell.
- Complete traceability on the label.
- Cut kept at a temperature between 0 and 4 °C from origin.

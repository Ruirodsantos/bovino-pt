Cooking is not "burning until it is done". It is steering a series of physical and chemical changes that happen at different temperatures. Whoever knows them chooses the doneness, instead of leaving it to chance.

## What changes with temperature

- **Around 40 to 50 °C**: the meat warms up, but is still raw inside.
- **Close to 50 to 55 °C**: myosin, one of the main proteins, begins to coagulate. The meat loses its translucency and begins to firm up.
- **60 °C and above**: collagen (the connective tissue) begins to shrink and expel liquid; the meat becomes firmer and loses juiciness.
- **Close to 65 to 75 °C**: actin, another protein, coagulates. The meat dries out and turns "like shoe leather" if there is no fat or gelatine.
- **Above about 70 °C, for a long time**: collagen slowly converts into **gelatine**, and working-muscle meat becomes tender and unctuous. This is the principle of the stew.

Two practical conclusions:

1. **Tender steaks** (*lombinho*, *lombo* (loin)): cook briefly, so as not to pass the point at which the meat dries out.
2. **Working cuts** (*acém* (neck and shoulder), *pá* (shoulder), *chambão* (leg, shin), *rabo* (tail)): cook for a long time, slowly, in liquid, to give the gelatine time to form.

## The degrees of doneness (temperature at the centre of the cut)

| Doneness | Temperature at the centre | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Rare (*mal passado*; *bleu/rare*) | about 48 to 52 °C | Bright red, almost cold at the centre |
| Medium-rare (*ao ponto para mal*; *medium-rare*) | about 54 to 57 °C | Pinkish red, warm and moist; the doneness most chefs choose for quality meat |
| Medium (*ao ponto*; *medium*) | about 58 to 62 °C | Pink, still juicy |
| Well done (*bem passado*) | above 68 to 70 °C | Grey, firm, drier |

These values are those in everyday use in professional kitchens. Food-safety authorities, such as the USDA in the United States, recommend an internal temperature of 63 °C followed by a 3-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, and 71 °C for minced meat. The reason: in minced meat, bacteria from the surface end up mixed into the interior. In a whole steak, little or nothing passes into the muscle; it is the surface that is sterilised by the crust. Whoever serves rare meat to customers takes on that responsibility and should buy meat of controlled origin.

## The crust: the Maillard reaction

At the surface, when the temperature rises above about 140 °C and the meat is dry, the **Maillard reaction** takes place: sugars and amino acids react and create hundreds of aromatic compounds, the brown colour and the "grilled" flavour. That is why the meat is dried thoroughly before searing, and why a pan that is not hot enough, or is crowded with steaks, produces grey meat: the water that escapes prevents the surface from rising above 100 °C.

## Salt

Salting in advance (from 40 minutes to the day before, leaving the cut uncovered in the cold) lets the salt penetrate and dry the surface, which improves the crust. Salting right before the frying pan only seasons the surface. Salting shortly before putting it on the heat leaves the surface moist, which hinders the crust; this is a point to be tested in practice with the cut in question.

## Resting

After leaving the heat, the meat keeps cooking on the inside (by 3 to 5 °C in steaks, more in large cuts). Take it off the heat **a little before** the desired doneness and let it rest for 5 to 10 minutes in a warm place. The juices redistribute and do not run out when it is cut.

## Techniques

- **Wood or charcoal embers**: the most traditional in Portugal, with the *posta mirandesa* as its symbol. Strong fire, thick cut, turn few times, coarse salt at the end.
- **Cast-iron frying pan**: high heat, little fat, dry cut; add butter, garlic and thyme only at the end, with the heat lower.
- **Reverse sear**: thick cuts go first into a low oven (about 100 to 120 °C) until they are some 8 to 10 °C below the desired doneness, and only then seared over a strong flame. It gives an even doneness from edge to edge.
- **Stew (*estufado*)**: seared meat, then cooked slowly and covered in liquid (wine, stock) over a very gentle heat, for hours.
- **Low temperature and sous-vide**: a restaurant technique in which the cut cooks for many hours at a low, controlled temperature. Safety does not depend on temperature alone: what counts is the temperature actually reached at the centre, the exposure time, the thickness, the preparation (whole cut or minced), the storage before and after, and any subsequent treatment. Do not use generic temperatures as a guarantee: follow time and temperature combinations validated by official food-safety sources (for example, USDA/FSIS tables, to be checked) and, in catering, the establishment's HACCP plan. Conventional cooking, with the cut measured at the centre with a probe, is a different case.

## Thermometer

In a demanding restaurant the doneness is not guessed. Use a probe thermometer and measure at the centre, without touching the bone.

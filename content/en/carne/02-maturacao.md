In Portuguese one speaks of *maturar* or *curar* meat. It means letting the meat rest under controlled cold after slaughter so that it becomes more tender and more flavourful. A cut that has just been slaughtered and butchered is tense and short on flavour. Given time, it changes.

## What happens inside the muscle

After slaughter the muscle enters rigor mortis and stiffens. Then the animal's own enzymes (calpains and cathepsins, among others) begin to break down the proteins that keep the fibres rigid. The meat **tenderises**. At the same time, proteins and fats break down into smaller molecules — free amino acids, fatty acids, aromatic compounds — and the flavour becomes deeper and more complex.

This happens in every kind of ageing. What distinguishes the methods is what happens at the surface.

## Two methods

### Vacuum ageing (wet aging)

The cut is vacuum-packed and kept cold. It loses no water, forms no crust and nothing is wasted. It tenderises well; the flavour develops into something more acidic and "bloody". This is the industry's method because it is safe, cheap and predictable. It usually lasts from one to a few weeks.

### Dry ageing (dry aging)

The cut is left **unpacked**, in a chamber with controlled temperature, humidity and air circulation. A dry crust forms at the surface which, at the end, is trimmed off and discarded. Inside the cut, water evaporates, which **concentrates the flavour** and allows the enzymes to work.

The usual conditions described in the technical literature are cold close to 0 to 4 °C, moderate to high relative humidity (in the order of 75 to 85 %) and constant air circulation. These ranges must be confirmed in primary sources before any professional use; see the notice at the end.

Ideal cuts: those with **bone** and **a cover of fat**, such as a bone-in high loin (*costeleta*, the bone-in rib steak). The cover fat and the bone protect the muscle from excessive drying.

## How long

| Time | What you notice |
|---|---|
| Up to 14 days | Tenderising. Flavour still close to fresh. |
| 21 to 45 days | The most common range in restaurants. Aroma of walnut and butter; more tender meat and more intense flavour. |
| 60 days or more | Very pronounced flavour, notes of cured cheese, ripe fruit and broth. No longer to everyone's taste. |
| Around 100 to 160 days | The territory of a few restaurants in the world, using meat from mature animals with plenty of fat. |

The restaurant **El Capricho**, in Jiménez de Jamuz (León, Spain), is the Iberian reference for long ageing. The Michelin Guide mentions ageing "of more than 60 days"; the *Diario de León* mentions up to 160 days. It works with oxen it raises itself, of native breeds, selected by chef José Gordón. Learning from this example is not about copying timings: it is about understanding that very long ageing demands a rigorous selection of cuts and especially controlled storage conditions. From what the press describes, El Capricho works with adult animals well covered in fat, and good fat cover helps limit losses through drying out; this is not a rule that the source states as absolute.

## The hidden cost

A dry-aged cut loses weight: water evaporates and then the crust is trimmed away. In long ageing the loss of yield is a considerable fraction of the cut (figures of 10 to 30 % or more are cited in studies, but they vary with the time and the cut). This is why aged beef is expensive: the customer pays for what was lost, for the chamber space and for months of tied-up capital.

## Safety

- The chamber must be dedicated, clean and with monitored temperature.
- Meat that has already been cut into steaks or minced is never aged. Ageing applies to whole cuts.
- Moulds that form at the surface (some are desired in the tradition) are cut away with the crust. The interior must not smell sour or of ammonia.
- At home, do not improvise. A domestic refrigerator lacks the humidity and air the process requires, and opening the door spoils everything.

## What to look for in a top restaurant

1. **The right animal**: age, breed, rearing.
2. **The right cut**: bone, fat, size.
3. **The right chamber**: stable temperature and humidity, records.
4. **The right time** for that cut, decided by someone who knows it.
5. **Cutting and cooking** worthy of months of work (see [cooking](../04-cozedura/)).

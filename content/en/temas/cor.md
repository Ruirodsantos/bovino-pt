scenes:
- title: What colour should the meat be?
  text: 'The colour of beef comes mainly from **myoglobin**, the protein that stores oxygen inside the muscle. And it changes with the chemistry at the surface: the same cut goes through three colours.'
  diagram: myoglobin
- title: 'Purplish red: just cut open.'
  text: 'This is the colour straight after cutting, when the myoglobin has not yet met oxygen. A cut that has just been opened, or that came vacuum-packed, has this colour.


    **It is not a defect.**'
  fig: cor-cena
- title: 'Bright red: the colour of fresh meat.'
  text: A few minutes after the cut is exposed to air, the myoglobin takes up oxygen and turns cherry red. It is the colour we associate with fresh meat.
- title: 'Brown: left too long in the air.'
  text: 'When the iron in the myoglobin oxidises, the meat turns brown. In a refrigerated display this happens after a few days.


    Brown on the surface does not, in itself, mean the meat has gone off: it means it has been exposed for a long time.


    Smell and texture can reveal signs of spoilage, but they cannot, on their own, confirm that the meat is safe. The absence of a bad smell is no guarantee: safety depends above all on the cold chain and the use-by date.'
- title: And why is beef darker?
  text: Because it has more myoglobin than pork, veal or poultry. This is described in meat-science textbooks such as *Meat Science and Nutrition* (eCampus Ontario / BCcampus).
- title: And in a restaurant?
  text: The meat often comes vacuum-packed or aged, and the colour on the surface is misleading. What you judge is the **freshly cut interior**, after a few minutes in the air.
- title: 'The warning sign: dark, firm and dry.'
  text: 'When the animal arrives at the abattoir tired or stressed, it uses up the glycogen reserves in its muscle. Without glycogen, the pH of the meat does not fall as it should after slaughter.


    The result is **DFD** meat (*dark, firm, dry*), known in the trade as a *dark cutter*. It is dark, sticky on the surface, does not develop a bright red colour and keeps less well.


    For a demanding restaurant, it goes back.'
note: The statements on myoglobin colour and DFD meat were checked against the cited textbook; the rest await independent review.

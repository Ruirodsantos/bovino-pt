- title: 'Ageing: letting the meat rest.'
  text: 'In Portuguese people talk of «maturar» or «curar» meat. It means letting it rest under controlled cold after slaughter so that it becomes more tender and more flavourful.


    A freshly slaughtered, freshly cut piece is tense and short on flavour. **With time, it changes.**'
- title: What happens inside the muscle?
  text: 'After slaughter the muscle goes into *rigor mortis* and stiffens. Then the animal''s own enzymes (calpains and cathepsins, among others) begin to break down the proteins that keep the fibres rigid. The meat **tenderises**.


    At the same time, proteins and fats break down into smaller molecules (free amino acids, fatty acids, aroma compounds) and the flavour becomes deeper and more complex.


    This happens in every kind of ageing. What sets the methods apart is what happens at the surface.'
- title: 'Vacuum: industry ageing.'
  text: 'In vacuum ageing (*wet aging*) the piece is vacuum-packed and kept cold. It loses no water, forms no crust and nothing is wasted.


    It tenderises well; the flavour develops towards something more acidic and «bloody». It is the industry''s method because it is safe, cheap and predictable. It usually lasts from one to a few weeks.'
- title: 'Dry: restaurant ageing.'
  text: 'In dry ageing (*dry aging*) the piece is left **unwrapped**, in a chamber with controlled temperature, humidity and air circulation. A dry crust forms on the surface, which is trimmed off and thrown away at the end. Inside the piece, water evaporates, which **concentrates the flavour** and lets the enzymes do their work.


    The usual conditions described in the technical literature are cold close to 0 to 4 °C, moderate to high relative humidity (in the region of 75 to 85 %) and constant air circulation. These ranges should be confirmed against primary sources before any professional use.


    Ideal pieces: with **bone** and a **covering of fat**, such as a bone-in high loin (rib steak, *costeleta*). The fat and the bone protect the muscle from drying out too much.'

- title: How long?
  text: '| Time | What you notice |

    |---|---|

    | Up to 14 days | Tenderising. Flavour still close to fresh. |

    | 21 to 45 days | The most common range in restaurants. Aroma of walnut and butter; more tender meat and a more intense flavour. |

    | 60 days or more | Very pronounced flavour, notes of cured cheese, ripe fruit and broth. Not to everyone''s taste. |

    | Around 100 to 160 days | The territory of a few restaurants in the world, using meat from mature animals with plenty of fat. |'
- title: The Iberian benchmark.
  text: 'The restaurant **El Capricho**, in Jiménez de Jamuz (León, Spain), is the Iberian benchmark for long ageing. The Michelin Guide mentions ageing «of more than 60 days»; the *Diario de León* mentions up to 160 days. It works with oxen it raises itself, of native breeds, selected by chef José Gordón.


    Learning from this example is not about copying timescales: it is about understanding that very long ageing demands rigorous selection of pieces and especially well-controlled storage conditions. From what the press describes, El Capricho works with adult animals well covered in fat, and a good covering helps limit losses from drying out; the source does not state this as an absolute rule.'
- title: Why does it cost so much?
  text: 'A dry-aged piece loses weight: water evaporates and, afterwards, the crust is cut off. In long ageing the loss of yield is a considerable fraction of the piece (figures of 10 to 30 % or more are cited in studies, but they vary with the time and the piece).


    That is why aged meat is expensive: the customer pays for what was lost, for the chamber space and for months of tied-up capital.'
- title: And safety?
  text: '- The chamber must be dedicated, clean and have monitored temperature.

    - Meat that has already been cut into steaks or minced is never aged. Ageing applies to whole pieces.

    - Moulds that form on the surface (some are wanted in the tradition) are cut away with the crust. The inside must not smell sour or of ammonia.

    - At home, do not improvise. A domestic fridge lacks the humidity and the airflow the process needs, and opening the door spoils everything.'
- title: What do you look for in a top restaurant?
  text: '1. **The right animal**: age, breed, rearing.

    2. **The right piece**: bone, fat, size.

    3. **The right chamber**: stable temperature and humidity, records.

    4. **The right time** for that piece, decided by someone who knows it.

    5. **Cutting and cooking** worthy of months of work.

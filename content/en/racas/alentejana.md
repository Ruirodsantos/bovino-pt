## The history

The Alentejana is the mainland south's breed par excellence. For a long time it was, above all, a working animal: it pulled ploughs and carts in a farming system that depended on the strength of oxen. With mechanisation and growing demand for meat, the second half of the twentieth century brought the temptation to replace it or cross it with foreign breeds, in a race for faster-growing animals. The breed held out, and today it is raised chiefly for meat.

## How to recognise it

The coat is red, from a dark shade (retinto) to a lighter one (trigueiro), with no white or black hairs mixed in. The most characteristic feature is the dewlap, the fold of skin under the neck, with several folds. According to the catalogue, it is an adaptation to heat: it increases the surface through which the animal cools itself. The horns are an off-white, with almost black tips.

## How it is raised

The breed's homeland is the Alentejo, with a continental climate of wide temperature swings, little rain concentrated in winter, and long, dry summers. The poor pastures led the breed to develop a capacious digestive system and a robust skeleton. The catalogue notes that, since it began to be raised solely for meat, its conformation, formerly very unbalanced towards the forequarters, has been improving.

## Today

According to the catalogue's 2019 data, it is the native cattle breed with the most females registered in the herd book (8,362, against 8,358 for the Mertolenga, a minimal difference), and the one with the strongest presence in the districts of Portalegre, Évora and Beja.
